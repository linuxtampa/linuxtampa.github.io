Why my client automated Let's Encrypt instead of asking its customers to edit DNS

November 18, 2026 aws acm letsencrypt tls automation devops

Let's Encrypt proves you control a name by serving a file from your web server; AWS ACM wants a DNS record only the customer can add. Why that difference made one of my clients automate renewals instead of chasing customers.

One of my clients runs a SaaS platform on AWS. Some of its products can be white-labeled: a customer points a hostname of their own at the client's load balancer (dashboard.customer-example.com, say), and the platform serves it with that customer's branding. The client also offers its services under its own domains and branding, which turn out to be a different story (the end of this post explains why). The white-label hostnames were on Let's Encrypt certificates that someone had imported into AWS Certificate Manager (ACM) by hand. They were all due to expire within a few days of each other, and ACM does not renew imported certificates.

The obvious fix was "just let ACM issue them". The client tried it. It did not work, and why it did not work turned out to be the interesting part.

Two ways to prove you own a name

Every public CA has to check that you control the name before it signs a certificate. ACM and Let's Encrypt do that check differently.

ACM (public certificates) Let's Encrypt
How you prove control Publish a DNS CNAME record that ACM gives you (or answer an email) Serve a small file over plain HTTP at /.well-known/acme-challenge/<token> (HTTP-01), or publish a DNS TXT record (DNS-01)
Who has to act Whoever controls the domain's DNS Whoever controls the web server the name points at
Renewal Automatic, as long as that CNAME stays published Your job: certificates last 90 days, and your ACME software renews them
Time limit A pending request times out after 72 hours None; every renewal is a fresh challenge

Look at the second row. For ACM, the proof lives in DNS. For Let's Encrypt's HTTP-01, the proof lives on the server. Here are the two flows side by side, with the same customer hostname in both.

ACM with DNS validation: the proof needs the customer.

Let's Encrypt with HTTP-01: the proof needs only the client.

The first diagram has two humans and a deadline in the middle of it. The second has none.

Why that mattered for my client

For a white-labeled hostname, the customer owns the DNS and the client owns the server. The customer already did the one thing that was needed: they pointed their hostname at the platform. That is exactly what HTTP-01 relies on. If dashboard.customer-example.com reaches the client's load balancer, the client can answer a challenge for it, and nobody outside the client's company has to do anything.

ACM wanted the opposite. For each of those hostnames, a person at the customer had to log in to their DNS provider, add a CNAME with a long machine-generated name, and leave it there forever. All of the requests were made and then waited on. The 72 hours ran out, and every one failed with VALIDATION_TIMED_OUT. It was not that anyone refused. These are small businesses, the person who owns DNS is often an agency or a registrar login nobody remembers, and "please add this record" has to survive an email chain. The client was about to spend a week of account-manager time to avoid writing a script.

The deadline and the arithmetic settled it. Coordinating that many outside parties before a fixed date has a high chance of missing one. Automating a renewal the client fully controls has a low one. So the client stopped chasing customers, and I wrote the renewer.

What I built

The design is small, and almost all of it is deliberately boring.

  1. A tiny, stateless responder. An HTTP-01 answer is <token>.<thumbprint of your ACME account key>. The thumbprint is public and fixed, and the token is in the request URL. So a function can answer any token with no database and no storage:

    def handler(event, context):
    token = challenge_token(event["path"])          # validates the URL shape
    if event["httpMethod"] not in ("GET", "HEAD") or not token:
        return {"statusCode": 404, "body": ""}
    return {"statusCode": 200, "headers": {"Content-Type": "text/plain"},
            "body": f"{token}.{os.environ['ACME_ACCOUNT_THUMBPRINT']}"}

    It runs as an AWS Lambda behind the load balancer. A rule on port 80 sends /.well-known/acme-challenge/* for the client's hostnames to it, and everything else keeps redirecting to HTTPS.

  2. A Python renewer that asks ACM how long each certificate has left, orders a new one from Let's Encrypt if it is inside a 30-day window, and imports the result to the same ACM certificate ARN. Re-importing to the same ARN is the important trick: the load balancer's listener keeps pointing at the same certificate object, so nothing about the load balancer changes.

  3. A scheduled CI job (a GitLab pipeline schedule, once a week) that runs the renewer with short-lived credentials, then posts one summary message to the client's team chat: renewed, or failed and why. No servers, no cron box, nothing to patch.

There is one shared Let's Encrypt account for all of the names. The account key lives in a secrets manager and never in the repository. Let's Encrypt rate limits are per name, not per account, so one account is fine.

What the real run taught me

Mocks told me the ACME flow worked. Production taught me three things the mocks could not.

  • ACM insists the key type matches on re-import. The first import failed because the renewer generated RSA-2048 keys and the existing certificates were RSA-4096. Nothing changed (a failed import is a no-op), and the renewer now reads the current key size and matches it.
  • A load balancer is several machines. Right after an import, my own check saw the new certificate while a second check a few seconds later still saw the old one. The renewer now resolves every address of the host and waits until all of them serve the new serial.
  • Test against staging first, and make staging unable to hurt you. Let's Encrypt has a staging environment with the same API and no rate-limit pain. The renewer defaults to staging, and a staging run is hard-coded never to import anything. I did the first handful of hostnames by hand, in order of least traffic, then let the schedule do the rest unattended at 2:33 AM. It did, and the chat message was waiting when I woke up.

When to use which

Here is how I think about the choice now:

This is not "ACM bad, Let's Encrypt good". ACM is still the better answer when you control the DNS (your own domains, your own zones): you add the CNAME once, and renewal is free and invisible forever. That is why my client's own-brand domains never had this problem: it controls their DNS, so ACM issues and renews those certificates with nobody lifting a finger.

HTTP-01 is the better answer when the party who controls DNS is not the party running the server, which is the white-label, reseller and multi-tenant situation. Its limits are real: it only works for names that actually point at your server, it cannot issue wildcards (that needs DNS-01), and you now own a renewal pipeline and its alerting. And certificate lifetimes are heading down across the whole industry, so "automate it" stops being optional anyway.

The takeaway

Pick the validation method by asking who can already do the thing the proof requires. ACM's DNS proof needs the customer. Let's Encrypt's HTTP-01 proof only needs to show that you can answer a challenge via HTTP. My client chose the one that did not depend on other people's calendars. It took about 450 lines of Python and a 40-line Lambda, and the certificates now renew themselves.
